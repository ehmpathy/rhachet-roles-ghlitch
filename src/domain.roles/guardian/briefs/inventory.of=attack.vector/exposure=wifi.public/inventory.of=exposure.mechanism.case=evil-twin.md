# inventory.of=exposure.mechanism · case=evil-twin

## .what

the **exposure.mechanism** node for `case=evil-twin` — *how* a rogue access point turns a person's
join of "public wifi" into harm. this is the weakness that lets the exposure become a compromise,
plus the **elimination** that breaks it.

> its output is signal for a person or a qualified security professional, never a substitute for one.

> **recommendation disclaimer.** the guidance below is offered to the best of our knowledge from the
> info found in research — it carries **no guarantee of success or outcome**, and must be reviewed by
> a qualified security professional against your own real network, device, and threat model before
> reliance. it is groundwork for that review, not a substitute for it.

## .the mechanism

the weakness is that **802.11 ssids are unauthenticated**. a client device recognizes a network by
its name (ssid), not by a verified identity — so anyone can stand up an access point that advertises
a trusted name, and a nearby device cannot tell the fraud from the real one.

CISA states the adversary's move plainly:

> "In an evil twin attack, an adversary gathers information about a public network access point, then
> sets up their system to impersonate it. The adversary uses a broadcast signal stronger than the one
> generated by the legitimate access point; then, unsuspecting users connect using the stronger
> signal." — CISA, *Securing Wireless Networks* [S1]

MITRE's ATT&CK catalog names the same primitive — ssid impersonation plus a signal or access lever to
coerce the join:

> "By using a Service Set Identifier (SSID) of a legitimate Wi-Fi network, fraudulent Wi-Fi access
> points may trick devices or users into connecting to malicious Wi-Fi networks. ... Adversaries may
> provide a stronger signal strength or block access to Wi-Fi access points to coerce or entice
> victim devices into connecting to malicious networks." — MITRE ATT&CK T1557.004 [S3]

a second, quieter path is the **preferred network list (pnl) probe**: a device advertises the names
it has joined before, and a rogue ap answers to whichever name it hears:

> "adversaries may also listen for client devices sending probe requests for known or previously
> connected networks (Preferred Network Lists or PNLs). When a malicious access point receives a
> probe request, adversaries can respond with the same SSID to imitate the trusted, known network. ...
> Victim devices are led to believe the responding access point is from their PNL and initiate a
> connection to the fraudulent network." — MITRE ATT&CK T1557.004 [S3]

the tools are commodity, which is why the mechanism is common, not exotic:

> "The hacker then takes note of the legitimate network's Service Set Identifier (SSID) and sets up a
> new account with the same SSID. They can use almost any device to do this ... They may use a device
> called a Wi-Fi Pineapple to achieve a broader range. Connected devices can't distinguish between
> genuine connections and fake versions." — Kaspersky, *Evil Twin Attacks* [S7]

the geometry is radio range — the ap does not need to be near the victim's own equipment, only within
broadcast reach:

> "The typical indoor broadcast range of an access point is 150–300 feet. Outdoors, this range may
> extend as far as 1,000 feet." — CISA, *Securing Wireless Networks* [S1]

Wikipedia frames the resulting fraudulent ap, and MDN gives the canonical instance of a person who
joins one at an airport:

> "An evil twin is a fraudulent Wi-Fi access point that appears to be legitimate but is set up to
> eavesdrop on wireless communications." — Wikipedia, *Evil twin (wireless networks)* [S5]

> "A few weeks later, the user is at the airport and decides to use the free Wi-Fi. But unknowingly,
> they connect to a rogue access point running on an attacker's laptop." — MDN, *Strict-Transport-Security* [S6]

NIST frames why the join matters structurally: the client device is one of the wlan components whose
security governs the whole link, and here that component has associated to a hostile ap:

> "The security of each WLAN is heavily dependent on how well each WLAN component—including client
> devices, access points (AP), and wireless switches—is secured throughout the WLAN lifecycle." —
> NIST SP 800-153 [S2]

## .the answer axes

- **eliminate** — the mechanism is broken when confidentiality does not rest on the network's honesty.
  route sensitive work over cellular / a personal hotspot; where wifi is used, a trusted vpn encrypts
  *above* the malicious gateway so the rogue ap carries only ciphertext (see `case=evil-twin`
  symptom.mechanism, and Cloudflare on-path [S4] / Kaspersky [S7]). turn off ssid auto-join so a device
  does not silently answer a pnl probe. confirm the network name with venue staff before you join —
  CISA: "Always confirm the name and password of a public Wi-Fi hotspot prior to use" [S1].
  **verdict:** the *join* to an unauthenticated ssid cannot be eliminated at the person's layer (the
  protocol permits it) — so this node is **mitigated, not eliminated**; the elimination lives
  downstream, at the transport layer (tls/vpn), where the compromise is actually stopped.
- **detect** — the observable signs live in the symptom.expression brief (a cert alert, a wrong-domain
  captive portal, a later login-from-unknown-location alert).
- **respond** — if you suspect you joined a rogue ap, disconnect, halt all sensitive activity, and
  rotate credentials from a trusted device (see symptom.expression).

## .likelihood

**band: common.** the weakness (unauthenticated ssids) is universal to 802.11, the tools are commodity
([S7]), and high-density venues are named targets ([S3]). this is an ordinal judgment from the cited
sources, not a measured rate.
